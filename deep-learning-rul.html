Introduction to Deep Learning, Carnegie Mellon University, Spring 2026

Deep Learning for Engine Remaining-Useful-Life Prediction

With my teammate Harshit Jain, I compared three deep-learning architectures for predicting how many cycles a turbofan engine has left before it fails, on NASA’s C-MAPSS FD001 data: a stacked LSTM, a Transformer encoder, and a temporal convolutional network (TCN). We trained all three in PyTorch under one protocol, with three random seeds each, and wrote up the results as a paper.

  • 13.66 ± 0.47 cycles test RMSE for the Transformer, the most accurate of the three, which also had the lowest PHM-2008 score
  • Flipped ranking of the two best models between a single-seed run and the three-seed average
  • ρ = 0.99 agreement between the LSTM and the Transformer on which sensors matter

What we did

  • Built 30-cycle sensor windows with remaining useful life capped at 125 cycles: 14,241 training, 3,490 validation, and 100 test windows.
  • Trained an LSTM (56,641 parameters), a Transformer encoder (70,337), and a TCN (34,593) with Adam, a cosine learning-rate schedule, gradient clipping, and early stopping, three seeds each, on a Colab T4 GPU.
  • Evaluated with RMSE, MAE, and the asymmetric PHM-2008 score, by life stage, and against training-set size.
  • Measured per-sensor importance with gradients to compare what each model relies on.

Training under one protocol

Every model saw the same windows, the same optimizer and schedule, and the same three seeds, so the differences come from the architectures. The Transformer converged fastest, the TCN plateaued earliest, and the LSTM kept improving slowly across the full schedule.

One run is not enough on this benchmark: in a preliminary single-seed run the LSTM looked clearly better than the Transformer (13.55 against 14.14 RMSE), but across three seeds the order flips and the gap is small.

How much data each model needs

Trained on less data, the models rank differently. With 10% of the training windows the TCN was far better (RMSE 23.6 against about 71 for the other two); the Transformer improved sharply between 25% and 50% of the data, and the LSTM gave the best full-data result in this single-seed sweep (14.05).

Which sensors each model relies on

Gradient-based importance shows the two most accurate models also agree on what matters: the LSTM and Transformer rank the sensors almost identically (Spearman ρ = 0.99), while the TCN diverges (ρ ≈ 0.7).
