Prosthetic Foot

I designed a prosthetic foot whose forefoot can tilt from side to side, as a human foot does on uneven ground, and built a test bed that loads it repeatably. With Ronak Vyas, under Prof. Vineet Vashista in IIT Gandhinagar’s Human-Centered Robotics Lab, I took the design through three CAD iterations, each refined with finite element analysis in Ansys.

The problem

A foot has to carry around 0.8 times body weight in walking and up to 2.75 times in running, cushion the impact at heel strike, and still let the forefoot tilt inward and outward (inversion and eversion) on uneven ground. A prosthetic foot also has to fit a standard pylon mount, stay light, and survive years of daily steps. We set out to design one for forefoot mobility, a weight of 600–700 g, and a life of up to a million load cycles.

What I built

I designed the foot in SolidWorks over three iterations. The first, inspired by the human foot, was a single piece with four slits in the forefoot for inversion and eversion, but it was thicker than the pylon mount. The second matched the back of the foot to the mount’s width, cut the slits to two, and split the foot into four parts that are easier to make and assemble. In the third we worked on fewer forefoot fixtures and a lower weight. I also designed and built a test bed that applies repeatable loading to the mounted foot, so designs can be compared under the same conditions.

Testing and results

FEA in Ansys checked each iteration. On the first it showed that four forefoot slits weakened the structure without improving inversion or eversion, and that the foot bent near the pylon mount. Stress results on the second led to a lower profile. On the third we compared materials, carbon fiber, POM, and PPS, for energy storage, deformation at mid-stance, and impact absorption at heel strike. The test bed lets each design be loaded the same way, again and again, on the bench.

Why it matters

Affordable prosthetic feet are often rigid, which makes slopes and uneven ground harder to walk on. A foot whose forefoot can adapt to the ground, while staying light and durable, brings walking closer to natural.

Technical notes

Why slit the forefoot

Slits let each side of the forefoot bend on its own, so the foot can tilt inward or outward on a slope or uneven ground. FEA showed that four slits weakened the foot without adding that motion, so the design went to two.

What came next

Our poster planned FEA of the toe-off phase to choose the forefoot’s material properties, a stress-cycle test to check the foot lasts its intended life, and a physical prototype to validate the design.
