RAKSH

I led the five-person team that built RAKSH and developed its navigation. RAKSH is an autonomous robot for polyhouses: it drives along the crop rows, uses machine learning on its camera images to spot diseased plants, and sprays from a modular system that holds 10 chemicals. It qualified the Proof of Concept stage of Robofest-Gujarat 3.0, with a Rs 1,00,000 cash prize, and competed in the prototype stage.

The problem

Spraying pesticides inside a polyhouse, a plastic-covered greenhouse, is hot, repetitive work that keeps farm workers in close contact with chemicals, and blanket spraying uses more pesticide than the plants need. A robot that drives itself down the rows, checks the plants, and treats only where it is needed takes people out of that exposure.

What I built

I led the five-person team and owned navigation and perception. I developed the robot’s autonomous navigation using sensor fusion and vision-based crop-row detection, so it can follow the rows it sees with its camera. I designed the modular pesticide-delivery system: ten containers in a slide-in module, each with its own servo-driven valve feeding a shared line, so one robot can switch between treatments. I also designed a custom lead-screw linear actuator in the 3D-printed tower, which lifts the camera up to the plant’s leaves, and implemented the rocker-bogie suspension, which keeps the wheels on uneven ground. The robot runs on ESP32, Jetson Nano, and Raspberry Pi boards with ROS and Python.

How it works

Testing and results

We built and tested RAKSH from concept to a working prototype in under a year, including test runs outdoors on uneven grass. The team qualified the Proof of Concept stage of Robofest-Gujarat 3.0, winning a Rs 1,00,000 cash prize, and competed in the Level-3 prototype stage at Gujarat Science City, Ahmedabad, on December 29–31, 2023, in the Others Application Based Robot category.

Why it matters

Polyhouse farming is labor-intensive and chemical-heavy. RAKSH is designed to check plants regularly, catch disease early, and spray only the areas that need it, which cuts pesticide use and cost and keeps farm workers away from the chemicals.

Technical notes

Navigation

Crop-row detection gives the robot a visual reference to follow down each row. Fusing it with the robot’s other onboard sensors makes the estimate of where the robot is in the row more robust than vision alone.

Modular pesticide delivery

Each of the ten containers has its own servo-driven valve on a shared line, so the robot can switch treatments without changing hardware, and the whole module slides out of the frame.

Leading the team

As team lead I coordinated the five-person team from concept to the Robofest prototype stage, while owning the navigation and perception work myself.
