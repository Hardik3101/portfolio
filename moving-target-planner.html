16-782 Planning and Decision-Making in Robotics, Carnegie Mellon University, Fall 2026

Moving-Target Interception Planner

I wrote a C++ planner that catches a moving target at the lowest possible cost. A robot on a 2-D cost map of up to 1825 × 2332 cells knows the target’s whole trajectory and pays the cost of the cell it occupies at every time step. My planner treats this as a shortest-path problem over (cell, time) states, solves it exactly in the first call within a 0.9 s budget, and then replays the plan.

  • 9 of 9 test maps caught at the optimal cost, each matching an independent brute-force solver
  • 0.64 s to plan the hardest map, 4.3 million cells; at most 0.12 s on the other eight
  • 1,258 of 1,258 catchable random maps solved at the optimal cost in testing; the other 192 were correctly reported as uncatchable

What I did

  • Formulated the problem as a shortest path over (cell, time) states, measuring cost relative to the cheapest free cell so that waiting on the cheapest cells costs nothing extra.
  • Built three complete plans that later stages can only improve on: from breadth-first search, from Dijkstra on the reduced costs, and from a backward Dijkstra that lets the robot wait on any cell of the map.
  • Derived a lower bound that proves a plan optimal without further search, which settled five of the nine maps.
  • Wrote an exact search that recomputes only the cells whose neighbors changed, prunes with two admissible heuristics, and recovers the path from an undo log instead of a parent for every (cell, time) state.
  • Validated the planner against a brute-force solver on the nine maps and 1,450 random maps, with clean AddressSanitizer and UndefinedBehaviorSanitizer runs.

A plan that always exists, and a bound that can prove it optimal

Breadth-first search from the robot gives the fewest steps to every cell, so it shows exactly which catch times are possible; following its tree and waiting on the cheapest cell along the way gives a valid plan whenever the target can be caught at all. Dijkstra on the reduced costs gives a second plan and a lower bound for every catch time: the cheapest cell cost times the elapsed time, plus the smallest reduced cost of any path to where the target will be.

When the best plan meets that bound, it is optimal and planning stops. That happened on maps 1, 3, 4, 7 and 8. On the uniform-cost maps, the robot simply intercepts the target at the earliest possible time.

Waiting in the right place

Good plans often walk to a cheap cell, wait, and then intercept the target, but the first two plans can only wait on a cell of their own path. A third plan considers every cell of the map as the place to wait, using one backward Dijkstra seeded at the target’s position for every possible catch time, then one pass over all cells to pick the best. On a synthetic 2000 × 2000 map, the better of the first two plans was 5% above the optimum, while this one found the optimum exactly.

An exact search that touches only what changes

When the bound cannot settle it, an exact search takes over: dynamic programming over time layers, where each layer recomputes only the 3 × 3 neighborhoods of cells that changed in the previous layer. Reduced costs are what make this pay off: once the cheapest way into a region is known, its values stop changing. States that cannot beat the best plan so far are pruned with two admissible heuristics, and the path is recovered from a log of parent changes, so memory grows with the map and the number of changes rather than with the map times the time horizon.

On map 2, building the heuristic only from target positions the robot can reach in time cut the search from 19.3 million to 0.93 million cell updates. The exact search proved the plan optimal on maps 2, 5, 6 and 9.

Testing it

An independent brute-force solver that evaluates every (x, y, t) state, with no reduced costs, pruning or heuristics, agrees with the planner on all nine maps. On 1,450 random maps, from 5 × 5 to about 320 × 320 cells with costs up to 4 million, the planner caught the target in all 1,258 catchable cases at the optimal cost and reported the other 192 as uncatchable. Edge cases such as a 1 × 1 map and a 20,000-step trajectory matched too, and AddressSanitizer and UndefinedBehaviorSanitizer reported nothing.

With 2.1 s stalls injected into planner calls, the robot still caught the target. The limit I found: on 2000 × 2000 maps with smooth random costs, the exact search would need more than 10 s, so the planner uses its best plan when the budget runs out and cannot prove it optimal in time.
